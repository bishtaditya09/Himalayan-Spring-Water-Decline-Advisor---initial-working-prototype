Title: Spring Inventory and Springshed Mapping

Before any physical intervention, most successful spring revival programs in
Himalayan states begin with a systematic spring inventory: a village-by-village or
block-by-block survey that records each spring's location, approximate discharge,
seasonal behaviour, current usage, and perceived trend (increasing, stable,
declining, or dry) as reported by local residents who often have decades of
first-hand memory of how a spring has behaved.

Why this matters for AI-based approaches: local, informal knowledge -- "this spring
used to run all year but has been dry by April for the last five years" -- is
extremely valuable data that formal government datasets usually do not capture at
all. A well-designed decision-support tool should treat this kind of qualitative,
community-reported information as a first-class input alongside any available
rainfall or discharge measurements, not as an afterthought.

Basic spring classification used in several state springshed programs:
- Depression springs: emerge where the land surface intersects the water table,
  often in valley bottoms.
- Contact springs: emerge where a permeable rock layer overlies an impermeable
  layer, common in the sedimentary and metamorphic geology typical of large parts
  of the Himalayan belt.
- Fracture/fault springs: emerge along geological fractures or fault lines, and can
  be highly localized -- two springs a short distance apart may behave very
  differently depending on which fracture system feeds them.

Discharge measurement in the field: for low-flow springs, a simple bucket-and-stopwatch
method (time taken to fill a container of known volume) is commonly used and is
accurate enough for trend monitoring purposes, even without expensive flow-metering
equipment. Repeating this measurement on a fixed schedule (for example, monthly)
across the dry and wet seasons builds the kind of time-series record that makes
trend analysis and forecasting possible in the first place.

Community-based monitoring: training local volunteers, self-help groups, or school
students to take and log these periodic measurements has been used successfully in
several Himalayan states to build sustained monitoring datasets that would otherwise
require ongoing government field staff time.
