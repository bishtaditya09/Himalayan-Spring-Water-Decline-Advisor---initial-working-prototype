Title: Recharge Pits and Percolation Structures for Spring Revival

Recharge pits are one of the most widely used low-cost interventions for reviving
declining hill springs. The core idea is simple: instead of letting monsoon runoff
race downhill over hard or degraded slopes, you slow it down and give it time to
soak into the ground above the spring's recharge zone.

Where to dig: Recharge pits should be placed upslope of the spring, ideally within
its identified recharge area (the zone of higher elevation land whose subsurface
water eventually feeds the spring). Locating this area correctly is the single most
important step -- pits dug outside the true recharge zone will have little to no
effect on the spring's discharge, however well built. A rough hydrogeological survey
(even a simple slope-and-geology walk with a local expert) should precede construction.

Typical dimensions: A standard percolation pit is roughly 1 to 2 metres deep, 1 metre
wide, and 1 to 1.5 metres long, though this varies with soil type and available
land. Pits are usually staggered in rows along the contour of the slope so that water
overflowing one pit is caught by the next, rather than continuing downhill unchecked.

Construction notes:
- Pits should be filled loosely with boulders, gravel, and coarse sand in layers
  (boulders at the base, finer material on top) to prevent collapse while still
  allowing water to percolate through.
- A silt trap or small catch pit uphill of each recharge pit reduces siltation and
  extends the structure's working life -- these need periodic desilting, especially
  after the monsoon.
- Spacing between pits typically follows the contour at intervals of a few metres,
  adjusted to the slope gradient; steeper slopes usually need closer spacing to
  meaningfully slow runoff.

Expected outcomes and limitations: Recharge structures do not produce an immediate
increase in discharge -- the effect is usually seen only after one or two full monsoon
cycles, as recharged water takes time to travel through the subsurface to the spring.
Communities should be told this clearly, since expecting instant results is a common
cause of program abandonment. These structures also work best as part of a broader
treatment of the recharge area (see catchment protection notes) rather than as a
standalone fix.
