Title: Catchment (Recharge Area) Protection and Afforestation

A spring's health is only as good as the land above it. Deforestation, overgrazing,
unplanned construction, and road cutting in a spring's recharge area all reduce how
much rainwater infiltrates into the subsurface rather than running off the surface,
and this is frequently the underlying cause behind springs that were reliable for
generations suddenly declining within a decade or two.

Identifying the recharge area: this is not simply "the hill above the spring" in a
generic sense -- it requires tracing the local geology (rock type, fracture and
fault patterns, soil depth) to estimate which upslope area actually drains
underground toward the spring, since Himalayan aquifers are frequently perched and
localized rather than large uniform groundwater basins. Where formal hydrogeological
mapping is not available, a practical proxy is to work with the visible natural
drainage lines and slope above the spring, while being transparent that this is an
approximation.

Recommended interventions in the recharge area:
- Afforestation with native, deep-rooted species rather than fast-growing
  commercial monocultures (some non-native plantation species are associated with
  higher water uptake and reduced infiltration compared to native mixed forest).
- Restricting free grazing in actively treated recharge zones during the initial
  recovery years, since grazing compacts soil and reduces infiltration.
- Avoiding new construction, road cutting, or blasting within the mapped recharge
  zone where possible, since these can physically sever the subsurface flow paths
  feeding a spring -- a documented cause of sudden spring death in hill road
  construction areas.
- Terracing and bunding on any cultivated slopes within the recharge zone to reduce
  runoff velocity.

Long time horizon: unlike a recharge pit, forest and soil recovery in a catchment
area is a multi-year to multi-decade process. This intervention is the most
important for long-term spring health but the least visible in the short term, which
makes sustained community and institutional commitment essential rather than a one-time
project activity.
